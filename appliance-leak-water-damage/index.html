Appliance Leak Water Damage Help in Rochester

Nearly every appliance leak we handle comes from one of three sources. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. Dishwashers usually leak a little at a time, at the drain hose or door seal, hidden beneath the cabinets. The ice maker line on a refrigerator is a thin tube, and it can drip for months behind the unit. Cleanup is different for each of them.

On every Rochester job, the first step is moving the appliance and tracing the path the water took. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. With a laundry leak we look at the rooms next to it and the wall behind the washer, plus the ceiling below when the laundry sits on an upper floor. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

Your First Steps

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. If not, switch it off at the breaker instead.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineBehind a washer, on the wall, are separate hot and cold valves. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Mop Up What You CanGetting reachable water up with a mop or towels stops it from moving under the cabinets and the floor. Use a vacuum only if it is a wet and dry model.
  4. Leave the Door ShutA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. Opening the door mid-cycle can release the whole load of water onto the floor.
  5. Document What FailedKeep the part that failed, whether a hose, a fitting or a valve, and get photos of it. Both the appliance maker and your insurer may want to look at it.
Why It Happens

Why This Happens

  • Plastic or copper ice maker lines that kink, crack or loosen at the fitting.
  • Worn dishwasher door gaskets, cracked drain hoses or a failed pump seal.
  • A dishwasher that was installed out of level or with a supply connection left loose.
  • Aging rubber supply hoses on a washing machine, which weaken and then split under pressure.
  • Overloading the drum or adding excess detergent, either of which can make the machine overflow.
Why Speed Matters

What the Water Damages

Downstairs Ceiling Damage

Water from a second floor laundry room drops through the floor and collects on the ceiling below. Drywall there sags and stains while the insulation over it holds water.

Flooring and Subfloor

A leak runs under laminate, hardwood or vinyl plank flooring with little resistance. Boards cup or swell, and the subfloor underneath stays wet long after the surface looks dry.

Hidden Mold at the Back

Behind a refrigerator or a dishwasher it is warm and dark, and nobody checks there often. A slow drip there feeds mold on the wall and floor long before anyone notices.

Cabinet Damage

Dishwasher leaks soak cabinet sides and toe kicks from underneath. Panels swell and come apart, and a wet cabinet box can keep the wall behind it damp for weeks.

Coverage

What About Insurance?

A hose that bursts or a machine that overflows without warning is the kind of sudden and accidental event homeowners policies often cover. Payment typically goes toward the damage the water did, and the appliance or the part that failed is not included. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. Certain insurers will want to know the hose's age and whether it was kept up. Hold on to the part that failed, photograph everything ahead of cleanup, and review what your policy says.

Pricing

What Affects the Cost

  • Spread of the water into other rooms, and into the ceiling beneath an upstairs laundry if there is one.
  • Which appliance leaked and whether it was a fast flood or a slow seep.
  • Type of water, since gray discharge requires sanitizing and clean supply water does not.
  • Whether mold has started and has to be removed before the area can be dried.
